# Chapter 1: Homecoming

*Day 0 · Friday, September 25 · Merritt, Colorado*

---

Four days before the lights came on over the football field in Merritt, Colorado, something went into the water.

It went in a hundred and thirty river miles to the west and four thousand feet higher, through a storm drain under a hospital, in a city most people on the plains had driven through once on the way to somewhere prettier. Nobody saw it go. The Arklay River took it the way it took everything in September. The snowmelt was long gone and the channel ran low and cold over the granite. The river carried it down out of the canyon, past the reservoir and the bottling plant with the blue mountain on its sign, past the last gas station and the first subdivision, and out onto the flat.

At the edge of Denver the Arklay gave up its name to the South Platte. The South Platte is not a pretty river. It runs north out of the city past gravel pits and treatment plants and the backs of warehouses. At Greeley it bends east and spends the next two hundred miles crossing the plains, sandy and braided and shallow enough in autumn for a heron to stand in the middle of it.

At twenty minutes past eight on Friday night, the front edge of whatever had gone into the water four days earlier slid under the I-76 bridge at Merritt and kept going. That was about the time the Merritt Mustangs kicked off to the Cutler Coyotes.

Nobody saw that either.

⁂

The concession stand smelled the way it had smelled for Nora Mercer's entire life: burnt popcorn oil, cocoa powder, and the chemical cherry of the snow-cone syrup. Nobody had bought a snow cone since Labor Day, but the Booster Club put the syrup out every week on principle.

"Two hot dogs, a nacho, a Mountain Dew," Mateo said, sliding a five and a crumpled one through the window to her. "Mrs. Albers wants to know if the nacho cheese is the real cheese."

"There's no real cheese. There's never been real cheese."

"I told her that. She wants to hear it from management."

"I'm not management."

"You're wearing the apron." He leaned into the window so that the light from the heat lamp caught his face, which was sunburned across the nose from a week of moving pipe for his uncle. "Management wears the apron, Nora. That's how it works. That's capitalism."

She gave him the hot dogs and the nacho with its hot orange cheese and made change out of the cigar box. Out past the stand the field lights hummed over everything. The whole town was out there under them: the bleachers, the chain-link, the pickups nosed in along the fence on the east side with their tailgates down, the band in their maroon jackets, and the homecoming court in a row of borrowed convertibles at the thirty-yard line, waiting to be driven around the track at halftime. Past the lights, the dark came right up to the end zone. Beyond it was nothing but beets and corn and the long black line of cottonwoods marking where the river was.

Merritt had been playing football under those lights since 1987, when the sugar company and the co-op split the cost of the poles. There was a plaque about it on the press box. Every year someone suggested replacing it with a plaque that said something else, and every year it stayed.

"I'm going to write about this," Nora said.

"About the cheese."

"About the plaque. About how the most important thing that happened here in forty years is that someone bought lights."

"You already wrote your essay."

"I'm rewriting my essay."

Mateo took a nacho chip off the top of Mrs. Albers's order and ate it. "How many times is that?"

"It's due November first."

"How many times, Nora?"

"Four," she said. It was six.

The essay was six hundred and forty-nine words, one under the limit, and it was about leaving. The prompt said *Describe a place that has shaped who you are.* She had spent the summer describing Merritt the way you describe the walls of a room you're trying to get out of. It had a line in it she liked and was also a little ashamed of: *In Merritt, the river is the only thing that ever gets to go anywhere.* She had read it to her mother once, at the kitchen table. Her mother had been quiet for a second too long and then said *That's good, honey,* in the voice she used for patients.

The essay was going to CU Boulder. Boulder was an hour and forty minutes away, which was not far, which was the point. Nora didn't need to go far. She just needed to go somewhere the river went.

Out on the field, Cutler fumbled on their own twenty-two and the Merritt side came up off the bleachers in one long roar. Nora felt it in the plywood floor of the stand. Mateo turned to look, and she saw the back of his neck and the white stripe where his hat usually sat. For a second the whole night was so ordinary it hurt a little.

"Go," she said. "I've got the window."

"You sure?"

"Go watch your cousin drop a pass."

He went. She wiped the counter and watched him cross to the bleachers. Then she looked for her family, because she always did, the way you check your pocket for your keys.

Her mother was on the fourth row of the home side, where she always sat, in the maroon fleece with the hospital logo on it. She sat straight-backed and alert, the way nurses sit everywhere, even at football games. She had worked a twelve-hour day shift at Logan County and driven straight here, and you'd never know it. Next to her, Eli sat with his knees up and his atlas open on them. He wore the big over-ear headphones he used to cut the noise, and he had a pencil in his mouth.

Eli was ten. Most ten-year-olds at a football game watched the football, or the older kids, or their phones. Eli watched maps. He had a *National Geographic Atlas of the World*, eleventh edition, that he'd bought with his own money at the library sale in Sterling for four dollars. It was very nearly the heaviest thing he owned. He carried it everywhere in a canvas grocery bag and annotated it in pencil. Nora had caught him once correcting the population of Sterling in the margin. He had looked up the new census number and wanted the book to be right.

As she watched, her mother leaned toward Eli without taking her eyes off the field and said something. Eli took the pencil out of his mouth, dug his phone out of the grocery bag, looked at it, and held it up for her. Nora knew what was on the screen without seeing it: a number and an arrow. The number was his blood sugar, read every five minutes by the sensor stuck to the back of his arm. The arrow said which way it was going.

Eli had been diagnosed with type 1 diabetes three years ago, after a week of drinking water like he couldn't get enough. He had wet the bed twice at seven years old, then lost six pounds in a week, then gone so quiet and gray at the dinner table that their mother put down her fork and said *Get the car.* Nora still thought about that sometimes: how the first sign was the thirst, and how nobody noticed because it was August and everybody was thirsty.

Her mother looked at the phone and nodded. Eli went back to his map. Nora let out a breath she hadn't noticed holding.

Her father wasn't there.

That wasn't unusual in September. Ray Mercer farmed nine hundred acres on the south side of the river, with sugar beets, field corn and two hundred head of cattle. The beet harvest was ten days out, and every piece of equipment he owned had decided to break this month. He missed half the home games every fall and all of them in harvest. What was unusual was that he had said he'd be here, and said it twice, because it was homecoming and his daughter was a senior. Ray Mercer did not say things twice unless he meant them.

At the end of the first quarter, her phone buzzed in the apron pocket.

**Dad:** Running late. One of the new steers is off. Save me a hot dog.

She wrote back **off how**, then deleted it and wrote **k**. Then she saved him a hot dog, wrapped in foil, in the warmer with a sticky note on it that said RAY — DO NOT SELL.

⁂

He came at the start of the second quarter, in the canvas coat that smelled like diesel, cattle and cold air. He came around the back of the stand, knocked twice on the door and let himself in the way he had since she was a kid.

"There's my hot dog."

"That's two dollars."

"Put it on my tab."

"You don't have a tab."

"I've got the biggest tab in this town," he said. "I'm your father." He unwrapped the foil and ate half the hot dog in one bite. Then he leaned on the counter beside her and looked out at the field without really seeing it. That was how she knew something was wrong.

Her father was not a talker. He had a way of standing next to you that did the talking for him. He'd stand there long enough that you started telling him things you hadn't planned to. Nora had once confessed to denting the good truck's tailgate simply because he'd stood beside her at the kitchen sink for three minutes without saying anything. Tonight he wasn't standing that way. Tonight he was just standing.

"What's wrong with the steer?"

"Don't know."

"Is it sick?"

"It's drinking."

Nora waited. "Cows drink, Dad."

"Not like this." He finished the hot dog and folded the foil into a small square. He did that with everything: foil, receipts, the paper around his cheeseburgers. He folded it smaller and smaller until it wouldn't fold anymore. "Bought six head at the sale barn in Brush on Wednesday. Feeder steers, off a place down by Kersey. Good-looking calves. Five of them are fine. One of them's been at the tank since noon. I filled it at six, and when I came in to change, it was down to the float again. That's a hundred gallons."

"Is that a lot?"

"For one steer? In one afternoon?" He turned the foil square over. "A steer that size drinks maybe fifteen gallons a day. When it's hot. It's not hot."

"Did you call Doc Henning?"

"He's in Julesburg with a heifer. Said he'll be by in the morning. Said it's probably nothing. Said if it goes down, call him back." He put the foil square in his coat pocket. "I've got it penned up in the trap by the tank, away from the others. It's probably nothing."

"Okay."

"It's probably a kidney thing."

"Okay, Dad."

He looked at her then, and it was her dad again, crooked mouth and gray at the temples. He'd been sunburned in the same stripe across the nose as Mateo every summer of her life.

"How's the essay?"

"It's done."

"How many times is that?"

"Why does everyone—" she said, and he laughed, and it was all right.

⁂

At halftime they drove the homecoming court around the track in the borrowed convertibles. Nora left the stand to Mrs. Delgado and went to sit on the fourth row with her family, because that was also what you did.

Up close, her mother smelled like the hospital: hand sanitizer and the lotion they gave you to fix what the sanitizer did. Her mother put an arm around her without looking and kissed the side of her head. "Hi, baby."

"Hi. How was work."

"Work was work." Her mother's phone was already in her other hand, lit up, and she tilted it so Nora could see. "Your aunt."

It was a FaceTime call. On the screen, Aunt Carmen had her hair in a towel. She was sitting on the floor of her apartment in Denver with a roller bag open in front of her, and she was holding up two bras.

"Which one," Carmen said. "For Frankfurt."

"Oh my God," Nora said. "Hi, Tía."

"Hi, mija! Tell your mother which one. She won't tell me."

"I'm at a football game," her mother said.

"You're at a *high school* football game. That's different. There's no rules at a high school football game."

Carmen was June's baby sister, seven years younger. She had been a flight attendant for eleven years, the last five on international routes out of Denver. She was the only person in the family who had been to Europe, or Asia, or the ocean in any sense that counted. She sent Eli postcards from every city she flew to. They were pinned up in a line around his window frame like a second, smaller map: Frankfurt, Munich, London, Tokyo, Reykjavík, Paris, Frankfurt again. Nora thought she was the most glamorous person alive, and had never told her so.

"The black one," Nora said.

"See? Your daughter has taste." Carmen threw the other bra over her shoulder. "I'm on the Monday Frankfurt, back Thursday. You want anything?"

Eli had taken his headphones off. He leaned into the frame over their mother's shoulder. "A pretzel."

"A pretzel! That's it? I'm going to Germany and you want a pretzel?"

"A real one. From the airport. The big kind."

"One big airport pretzel for the professor, you got it." Carmen pointed at him through the screen. "What are you mapping tonight?"

Eli turned the atlas so she could see. It was open to Colorado, and he'd been drawing on the river with the pencil. "The South Platte. Did you know the water in the South Platte gets used seven times before it goes to Nebraska?"

"I did not know that."

"Farmers take it out and put it on the fields, and it soaks back into the river, and then the next farmers take it out again. Seven times. So the water that's in the river here was already in Denver and Greeley and Fort Morgan."

"That's disgusting," Nora said.

"It's *efficient*," said Eli.

"It's both, baby," said Carmen. Then she looked away from the screen at something in her apartment, a TV or a window, and her face changed. "Hey, June, you hear about the thing up in Raccoon City?"

"What thing?"

"I don't know. Something. My neighbor's a cop in Golden. They called everybody in about an hour ago, all his days off canceled. He said there's some kind of, like, riot up at the hospital, and Raccoon City PD asked for mutual aid and then just stopped answering." She shrugged. "He said they're closing 70 at Idaho Springs. I don't know. Probably some protest thing. Umbrella's got that big plant up there."

"Huh," said their mother, in the nurse voice. "You be careful."

"I'm going to *Frankfurt*, June. I'm going to be careful in Frankfurt."

"Be careful in Frankfurt."

"Love you, bye, black bra, bye, professor," said Carmen, and the screen went dark.

Nora's mother held the dark phone for a second. Then she put it in her fleece pocket and looked at the field, where the band was forming up for the halftime show. Nora saw her look past the band, past the end zone and the dark at the end of the lights, toward the west. From the fourth row you could see a long way at night. The highway ran out toward Brush and Fort Morgan, marked by the red blinking lights on the grain elevators. Beyond that, too far to see, was the city. Beyond that were the mountains. Tonight, where the mountains should have been, there was nothing, because it was night.

"Mom?"

"Hmm?"

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong." Her mother smiled at her and squeezed her shoulder. "Your brother needs to eat something. Go get him a pretzel from the stand before he makes Carmen fly one in from Germany."

Walt Ybarra had not been to a high school football game since 1971. He saw no reason to start now.

He had played in that one, against Haxtun. He was seventeen and a nose tackle, and he broke two fingers on his left hand on the second play. He finished the game with them taped together, and afterward the coach shook his other hand and said, *Son, you're either the toughest kid I ever coached or the dumbest, and I'm not sure it matters.* Walt had thought about that sentence off and on for fifty-five years. He had come to believe the coach was right, and that it didn't matter. He figured that settled football.

So on Friday nights in the fall, while his daughter and her family went to the game, Walt sat in the radio shack.

The shack was the back room of the bunkhouse, the little cinderblock house a hundred yards from the farmhouse where hired men had slept in the forties. Ray had fixed it up for him after Walt's wife died and he sold the place in Iliff. Walt had not asked for this and had not refused it. It had one bedroom, one bathroom, a kitchen with a two-burner stove, and a back room he had lined with pegboard and filled with radios.

The antennas were outside: a tall vertical on the roof and a long wire strung between the bunkhouse and the cottonwoods by the river. June had asked him once, carefully, whether that many antennas were strictly necessary. He had told her no, and that was the answer to most questions worth asking.

He had been a lineman for the Highline Electric co-op for thirty-four years. He had climbed poles in blizzards, restrung wire in the wind, and put power back on for farms that didn't always know his name. He had been a ham radio operator for longer than that. The two things were the same to him. They were both about the invisible stuff that ran between people, which nobody thought about until it stopped.

The wall behind the operating desk held a world map: a big Rand McNally, six feet across, faded blue at the oceans. Stuck into it were more than four hundred colored map pins, one for every station he had ever made contact with. Red for the United States, blue for Canada and Mexico, green for everywhere else. There was a green pin in Tristan da Cunha, a spot so small it wasn't on the map, so he had drawn it in with a ballpoint. There was a green pin on a ship in the Indian Ocean, and one in Antarctica, at McMurdo Station, from 1994. His grandson liked to stand in front of the map and count them. Eli had counted four hundred and eleven. Walt had counted four hundred and twelve. They were still negotiating.

Tonight he had the scanner going on the shelf above the rig, low, because he liked the company.

The scanner was a different animal from the ham rig. It didn't transmit, only listened, and what it listened to was mostly nothing: the sheriff's dispatch in Sterling, the fire department, the highway patrol on I-76, the sugar company's trucks, the cattle trucks, the farmers on their handhelds. Over the years he had programmed it to pull in more than it had any business pulling in. When the air was right he could get the state patrol clear over to the Front Range. Sometimes, on a cold night with the clouds low, he got a little of Denver. Once, for about twenty minutes during a blizzard in 2011, he'd heard the Raccoon City police department dispatcher clear as a bell. Then the weather shifted and he never heard her again.

He heard her again tonight.

He was on the ham rig at the time, on the Colorado evening net. Eleven guys and two women checked in from all over the state every Friday at eight to say their call signs and their locations, report their weather and pass along any traffic. Walt had checked in at 8:02 with his usual: *Merritt, Logan County, clear and fifty-four, winds light out of the south, no traffic.* He was half listening to the net control station in Pueblo reading the roll when the scanner changed its voice behind him.

It was the sound that made him turn. The scanner had a way of changing when it caught something far away: a thinness, a hiss under the voice, like a phone call from the bottom of a well. He turned the net down and the scanner up.

"—all units, all units, be advised, we are getting multiple reports from the area of Raccoon City General, multiple reports of assaults in progress in the parking structure and the east entrance, units responding use caution—"

It was a woman. It might have been the same woman as 2011; he couldn't have said. She was working hard to keep her voice flat, and she wasn't managing it.

"—twelve, what's your twenty—"

A man's voice came through, broken up: "—on Arklay Road at the barricade, we got a civilian vehicle through the barricade, driver says he's a, he's a *courier*, says he's got a medical delivery for the General, I told him nobody's going to the—"

"Twelve, let him go, we don't have—"

"—copy—"

Then a lot of voices at once, stepping on each other. Walt couldn't pick them apart. He heard *the lobby* and *they're not stopping*, and under it someone screaming, not into a radio but somewhere near one. The scream had the flat, far-off sound of a thing picked up by accident.

He reached for his logbook without looking and wrote the time: **20:47. RCPD dispatch, very weak.** Then he didn't write anything else, because the dispatcher was talking again.

"—is anybody on this channel? Any unit? This is dispatch, any unit, any unit, please—"

Nothing answered her.

"—any unit. We have. Okay. Okay, we have, the doors are—"

She stopped in the middle of the word. It wasn't the signal fading. Walt had listened to ten thousand signals fade, and this wasn't that. The carrier stayed up, and the hiss under it stayed steady. Somebody's hand was still on the transmit key for three full seconds after the voice quit, and in those three seconds there was a sound. Walt could not have described it afterward, and later he would try, many times, for many people. It was wet. It was close to the microphone. It was not a word.

Then the carrier dropped, and the scanner went back to scanning and found the Logan County sheriff telling somebody named Donny that the Morrisons' horse was loose on County Road 29 again.

Walt sat back in his chair.

On the ham rig, turned low, the Pueblo net control was saying: "—Raccoon City station, I've got you checked in at twenty-oh-three, you still with us, over?" The net waited. "Raccoon City station, net control, you still with us?"

Nobody was.

Walt sat for a while. Then he wrote in the logbook, under the time, in his square lineman's printing: **Something wrong in Raccoon City.** He looked at the sentence. It looked like the kind of thing an old man writes. He did not cross it out.

Then he got up and went out to the lean-to on the side of the bunkhouse. He checked the charge on the battery bank he kept for the radios, twelve deep-cycle batteries in a row on a wooden pallet. It was a habit and it was a superstition, the thing he did when the weather looked wrong or a storm was coming. There was no storm coming. The sky over the river pasture was clear and full of stars, and out beyond the cottonwoods the river was making its low, steady sound over the sand.

The batteries were full. He stood there anyway, with his hand on the top of one of them, and listened.

Down at the bottom of the pasture, in the pen by the stock tank, something was drinking.

Merritt won 28–21 in overtime on a quarterback sneak. The Merritt side came down out of the bleachers onto the field and the band played the fight song twice. Somebody set off a firework in the parking lot, and the county deputy watching from his cruiser pretended not to notice. Later, Nora would try hard to remember every piece of it. She would remember the firework and the fight song. She would remember Mateo's cousin crying on the fifty-yard line with his helmet off. She would remember her mother hugging her from behind in the crowd and saying *You smell like hot dogs*, and Eli standing on the bottom bleacher with his headphones on, spinning slowly to take all of it in, like a kid in a snow globe.

She would not remember the drive home, though it was the last ordinary drive of her life. That bothered her more than almost anything.

Here is what happened on it. Mateo came and found her in the parking lot and said a bunch of people were going down to the sandbar for a fire, the usual spot under the county road bridge. He asked if she was coming, and she said *Maybe*, which meant no, because she had to take Eli home. Her mother was going to stay and help the Booster Club count the cash box. Nora drove Eli home in the bad truck, a 2004 Silverado with a cracked windshield and a passenger door that only opened from outside. They took the frontage road along I-76 because it was faster than going through town.

It was seven miles. Eli fell asleep in the first two, with the atlas in his lap and his cheek against the cold window. Nora turned the radio down and drove with her phone in the cup holder. His numbers showed on the lock screen, and she checked them without meaning to: 132 and steady, arrow flat. She had never been asked to do that. She had just started doing it the week he came home from the hospital, and she had never stopped.

On the left, the interstate ran dark and mostly empty. A few trucks were going east with their lights on, and one car was going west fast. On the right were the fields, then the cottonwoods, then the river you couldn't see. Just before the Merritt exit, as it had for two years, the billboard came up out of the dark. It was the only lit billboard between Brush and Sterling. It was a picture of a mountain, very blue and very white, and a bottle of water with the same mountain on its label.

**ARKLAY SPRINGS**  
*Pure as the Mountains.*  
**An Umbrella Company**

Nora had driven past it maybe a thousand times. She did not look at it tonight, and it wasn't something she would have remembered anyway. It was just the billboard.

The farmhouse porch light was on. Her father's truck wasn't in the yard. Through the windshield she could see its headlights down at the bottom of the river pasture, a quarter mile off, pointed at the pen by the stock tank. Biscuit was barking.

Biscuit was their Australian shepherd, six years old, blue merle, with one brown eye and one blue one. She barked at a lot of things: the UPS truck, the wind, coyotes, the neighbor's horses, her own reflection in the dark glass of the storm door. So barking was not unusual. What was unusual was that she wasn't barking at the house, or the truck coming up the drive, or Nora. She was standing at the edge of the yard where the grass stopped and the pasture started, facing the river. Every bark came out of her low and flat, like she was trying to push something away from her with it.

Nora got Eli inside, which took some doing. He woke up enough to walk but not enough to be helpful, and he insisted on carrying the atlas himself. She got him to his room and made him check his number again. He grumbled and did, and it was fine. She pulled his shoes off, turned out the light and left the door cracked the way he liked. Around his window, the postcards from Aunt Carmen caught the yard light: Frankfurt, Munich, London, Tokyo.

Then she stood in the kitchen and listened to the dog.

She texted her mother: **home. E asleep, 128 flat. Dad still down at the tank.**

Her mother wrote back right away. She always did. **Thank you baby. Leaving here in 10.** Then, a second later: **Tell your dad to come in.**

Nora took the big flashlight off the hook by the back door and went out.

⁂

The pasture ran downhill to the river through grass that had gone gold and dry in August and stayed that way. It crunched under her shoes. The flashlight made a white circle on it that bounced as she walked. Biscuit fell in beside her as soon as she passed the yard edge. She had stopped barking, which was better and also worse. She stayed tight against Nora's left leg, closer than she ever walked, so close that Nora could feel her shaking.

"Hey," Nora said. "Hey, girl. It's fine. It's Dad."

Her father's truck sat with its headlights on the pen. The engine was off and the driver's door was open, and the dome light made a small yellow room of the cab. He was standing at the pen fence with his forearms on the top rail. He didn't turn around when she came up behind him. He said, "Nor," like he'd known she was coming, and maybe he had.

"Mom says come in."

"In a minute."

The pen was called the trap. It was a small square of pipe fence around the stock tank, where they sorted cattle and doctored them, and where a sick animal could be kept away from the others. The tank was a big round galvanized one, eight feet across and two feet deep. It was fed by a pipe from the old well at the edge of the pasture, the shallow one that drew from the gravel under the river. A float valve kept it full. Nora had heard that valve her whole life, a thin hiss when the tank dropped and the valve opened, then silence when it filled back up and shut. She heard it now. It was hissing. As she stood there it kept hissing, and it did not stop.

The steer was at the tank.

It was a good-looking animal, a black baldy, black with a white face, maybe seven hundred pounds. It stood with its front feet at the rim and its head down in the water to the eyes. It was drinking the way she had seen cattle drink ten thousand times, in long pulls you could see go down its throat one after another. That was the only thing about it that looked right. The pulls didn't stop. There was no pause to breathe or look around, the way cattle do. It just drank, pull after pull after pull, and the float valve hissed, and the tank never got any fuller.

Steam was coming off its back.

Nora saw that in the headlights and didn't understand it at first. It wasn't cold enough for steam, only fifty-something. But it was rising off the steer's shoulders and spine and flanks in a thin white haze, the way it came off the cattle in January. Then she understood. It wasn't the air that was cold. It was the steer that was hot.

"Fever," she said.

"Yeah."

"Like a bad one."

"Yeah."

The other five steers were in the big pen next to the trap. They were all bunched in the far corner, as far from the trap as the fence let them get, pressed into the pipe so hard it creaked. Their heads were up. None of them made a sound.

"How long has it been like that?"

"Since I got back." Her father's voice was very even. "I shut the valve off for a while around nine-thirty. To see."

"What happened?"

"It drank the tank dry. Then it tried to drink the bottom of the tank. It was licking the metal." He paused. "Then it started walking the fence, looking for more. I turned the valve back on."

In the pen, the steer lifted its head.

Water poured out of its mouth. It wasn't dripping the way water drips off a cow's muzzle when it looks up from a tank. It poured, in a sheet, down its chin and chest. The last pulls it had taken had not gone anywhere. They had come right back up. Its throat worked and worked and nothing went down. It still wanted more. Nora could see that it wanted more.

Then it turned its head and looked at them.

Nora had grown up with cattle. She knew what a cow's eyes looked like: big and dark and wet and mostly uninterested in you. She knew what a sick cow's eyes looked like, dull and half-shut. She knew what a scared cow's eyes looked like, with the whites showing all the way around. This was none of those. The steer's eyes were open all the way. They were clouded over, the dark gone milky, like water with a drop of paint in it. They were looking at her and her father. The steer wasn't scared. There was nothing in its face at all, except the one thing it wanted, and it wasn't them.

Biscuit made a sound Nora had never heard her make, a long, thin whine through her teeth.

"Dad."

"Go on up to the house, Nor."

She didn't move. Neither did he.

The steer took its front feet off the rim of the tank. It walked, stiff-legged, past the tank and past the float valve, which was still hissing, still filling. It didn't come toward them. It went to the other side of the trap, the far side, where the pasture sloped away through the dark to the cottonwoods and the river. There it put its white face against the pipe fence and pushed.

The fence was two-inch pipe, welded, set in concrete. Nora's great-grandfather had built it. It had held bulls. The steer leaned into it with its whole weight, head down, back legs digging, and the rail it was pressing on bowed outward in the headlights. The weld at the post groaned.

"What's it doing?"

Her father didn't answer.

"There's a whole tank right there," Nora said. "What is it doing?"

Behind them, up at the house, the porch light was on and her mother's car was turning in at the end of the drive. Out in the dark past the fence and the pasture and the cottonwoods, the river kept running over the sand, low and quiet, the way it always had. It was the only sound besides the hiss of the valve and the groan of the pipe.

"It wants the river," Nora said.

Her father looked at her. In the light from the truck, his face was a face she had never seen on him before. She couldn't name it yet. By Christmas she would know it well, on him and on everyone.

"It's got a whole tank," he said.

The weld gave.

The river didn't care, and it didn't stop.

It carried what was in it past the Mercer place and the county road bridge, where eleven kids from Merritt High were standing around a fire on the sandbar in their homecoming shirts, drinking beer out of cans and throwing the empties at the water. It carried it past Sterling and Iliff and Crook, under the Highway 385 bridge and along the edge of the feedlots at Proctor. All night it ran east in the dark, over the sand, toward Nebraska. The water had already been in Denver and Greeley and Fort Morgan. It would be used seven times.

At 5:52 the next morning, a hundred and thirty miles behind it, a white light came up over the Arklay Mountains. It lit up the whole western sky, and people as far away as Sterling saw it and thought it was the sun coming up in the wrong place.

By then the water was forty miles past Merritt.

The missile was on time. The river was faster.
